Phase 1 — Preprocessing

Data Ingestion, Tokenization & POS Tagging

The BabyLM Strict-Small track provides only 10M words of child-directed and written text — roughly the amount a child hears by age 13. Before we can train a language model on this data, every sentence must pass through a carefully orchestrated preprocessing pipeline. This stage converts unstructured text into the structured, aligned representation our multi-task architecture requires: each token must have both a subword identity (for BERT's MLM objective) and a word-level POS tag (for the syntax head).

Phase 2 — Masking Strategy

Pretraining Masking: The 80 / 10 / 10 Rule

BERT learns by playing a "fill-in-the-blank" game called Masked Language Modeling (MLM). During each training batch, 15% of WordPiece tokens are randomly selected as prediction targets. But rather than always replacing them with [MASK], BERT applies a three-way stochastic rule. This prevents the model from learning a shortcut ("only predict when I see [MASK]") and keeps its representations robust for downstream tasks where [MASK] never appears. The primary mechanism: the selected token is replaced with the special [MASK] symbol. The model must reconstruct the original token using only the surrounding bidirectional context. This forces the encoder to learn rich contextual embeddings — each position must encode enough information about its neighbors to recover any masked word.

Concrete example

Input: "the cats sat on the mat" → Suppose "cats" and "on" are selected (15%). With 80/10/10: "cats" → [MASK] (80% case), "on" → "happy" (10% random). The model must predict the original words at both positions.

Architecture — Encoder-Only

Bidirectional Attention vs. Causal Masking

Imagine reading a sentence with a blanked-out word. To guess the missing word, you naturally look at words both before and after the blank — that's bidirectional attention. Decoder models like GPT use causal masking: they process left-to-right, predicting each next word without peeking ahead (like writing a story one word at a time). BERT, as an encoder-only model, uses full bidirectional self-attention, letting every token attend to every other token in the sequence. This is why BERT excels at understanding tasks but cannot generate text autoregressively. Click on tokens below to visualize the difference.

Architecture — Alignment Layer

Subword → Word Average Pooling

Here lies a fundamental mismatch: BERT produces one hidden vector per subword token, but POS tags are defined per word. The word "cats" is split into ["cat", "##s"] — two vectors — yet it should receive a single POS tag (NNS). Our solution is a sparse pooling matrix P of shape [num_words × seq_len]. Each row contains the averaging weights for one word: if a word spans k subwords, each receives weight 1/k. Multiplying P × H produces one vector per word. Optimization — Loss Functions

Multi-Task Cross-Entropy Loss Aggregation

Our MultiTaskBERT model optimizes three objectives simultaneously during pretraining. Instead of training separate models for each task, all three loss signals flow through a shared encoder backbone, creating a richer gradient landscape. The POS tagging loss acts as a structural regularizer — it teaches the encoder to maintain syntactic awareness even while learning semantics. The weight α controls the relative importance of syntax vs. semantics. Drag the sliders below to explore how different weightings affect the total loss.

Ltotal = LMLM + LNSP + α · LPOS
Masked LM Loss

L_MLM = −(1/N) Σ log P(x_i | ctx)

Cross-entropy over masked subword positions. Measures how well BERT can reconstruct hidden tokens from bidirectional context — the core semantic learning signal.

Next Sentence Pred.

L_NSP = −[y·log(p) + (1−y)·log(1−p)]

Binary cross-entropy: given two sentences, is B the actual next sentence after A? Teaches the model discourse-level coherence and inter-sentence relationships.

POS Tagging Loss

L_POS = −(1/W) Σ log P(tag_j | h_j)

Word-level cross-entropy over POS tags. Acts as a syntactic regularizer — forces the shared encoder to maintain grammatical structure awareness during pretraining.

Gradients & Backprop

MultiTaskBERT Gradient Convergence

During the backward pass, gradients from all three task heads converge into the shared Base BERT encoder. The MLM and NSP gradients flow directly from their respective heads. The POS gradient takes a longer path: it first passes through the POS Classifier, then backwards through the pooling layer. Because the pooling operation is a matrix multiply, backpropagating through it uses the transpose:
∂L/∂Hseq = PT · ∂L/∂Hword

This means the pooling matrix's transpose acts as a gradient distributor, sharing word-level syntactic error signals back to each constituent subword. Click below to watch the animated gradient flow.

Worked Example: Gradient Flow for "cats" ▼
Step 1: Subword Splitting
The word "cats" splits into subwords ["cat", "##s"] at sequence indices 1 and 2. Its word-level POS tag is NNS (plural noun).
Step 2: Forward Loss Computation
The model computes MLM loss at the subword level (LMLM = 1.85) and POS loss at the word level (LPOS = 0.90 for the NNS tag prediction).
Step 3: POS Gradient at Word Level
The POS loss gradient flows through the classifier, producing a word-level error vector gword for the word "cats".
Step 4: Gradient Distribution via PT
Since "cats" maps to 2 subwords with pooling weights [0.5, 0.5], the transpose distributes equally: ∂L/∂H"cat" = 0.5 × gword and ∂L/∂H"##s" = 0.5 × gword. Both subwords receive the syntactic learning signal!
Layered Architecture

POS-Guided Vocabulary Restriction

Predicting 1-out-of-30,522 vocabulary tokens is an enormous search space. But grammatical constraints can dramatically narrow it. In "the [MASK] sat", the blank must be a noun — we know this from syntax alone. The Layered architecture exploits this: it first predicts the POS tag, then maps the tag to its allowed vocabulary subset (~5,000 nouns instead of 30,522 tokens), and overlays a log-space mask on the raw MLM logits. Invalid word logits are pushed to −∞, effectively zeroing their probability after softmax.

MaskedLogits = RawLogits + log( vocab_mask + ε )
Allowed token (noun)

vocab_mask = 1 → log(1) = 0 → logit unchanged. Words like "cat", "dog", "table" pass through normally.

Blocked token (verb/adj)

vocab_mask = 0 → log(ε) ≈ −∞ → logit crushed. Words like "sat", "happy" become impossible after softmax.

Entropy reduction

For a predicted POS tag of NN (singular noun), the effective vocabulary shrinks from 30,522 to ~5,200 — a 6× reduction in prediction entropy. This is especially powerful under the BabyLM Strict-Small constraint, where the model has seen far fewer training examples and benefits greatly from this syntactic prior.

Gradients & Backprop

Layered Dual Gradient Flow

In Soft Layered Masking, the vocabulary mask is computed as the dot product of predicted POS probabilities and the POS-to-vocabulary matrix — making it fully differentiable. This creates a remarkable gradient pathway: the MLM loss gradient doesn't just update the MLM head — it also flows backward through the logits combiner and into the POS classifier. The result is co-tuning: improving MLM prediction accuracy simultaneously teaches the POS classifier to make better syntactic predictions, even without direct POS supervision at that step. Click below to see both gradient paths.

Worked Example: Soft Masking Co-tuning ▼
Step 1: POS Probability Prediction
For "the [MASK] sat", the POS classifier predicts: P(Noun) = 0.85, P(Verb) = 0.10, P(Adj) = 0.05. The true word is "cat" (Noun).
Step 2: Soft Vocabulary Mask Construction
The mask is computed as: m = P(NN) × MNN + P(VB) × MVB + ... This produces a weighted blend of allowed vocabularies — nouns get mask values near 1.0, verbs near 0.10, adjectives near 0.05. After log-overlay, verb logits drop by ~2.3 nats.
Step 3: Dual Gradient Paths
The MLM loss depends on the mask, which depends on POS probabilities. By the chain rule, ∂LMLM/∂P(NN) ∝ ∂LMLM/∂y × MNN. This creates a gradient that flows from the semantic loss into the syntactic head.
Step 4: Co-tuning Effect
Since the correct word is "cat" (Noun), the MLM loss decreases when noun probability increases. The gradient naturally pushes P(Noun) higher — the semantic reconstruction loss is teaching the syntax classifier, even without explicit POS labels at this step!
Evaluation & Benchmarks

Linguistic, Semantic & Generative Evaluation

To comprehensively evaluate our pretrained models, we use a three-pronged benchmark suite. BLiMP tests zero-shot grammatical knowledge (can the model distinguish grammatical from ungrammatical sentences?). GLUE measures fine-tuning performance on downstream NLU tasks like sentiment analysis and paraphrase detection. Together, they reveal whether our multi-task and layered architectures produce representations that are both syntactically aware and semantically transferable.

What BLiMP measures

BLiMP contains 67 linguistic paradigms testing phenomena like subject-verb agreement, island effects, and anaphor binding. A model scores by correctly preferring "The dogs are running" over "The dogs is running" in zero-shot — no fine-tuning involved.

What GLUE measures

GLUE benchmarks real-world NLU: sentiment (SST-2), paraphrase (MRPC, QQP), textual entailment (MNLI, RTE), and similarity (STS-B). Higher scores mean the pretrained representations transfer more effectively to diverse downstream tasks.

Summary

Architecture Review & Key Takeaways

Our experiments demonstrate that combining multi-task pretraining with structural syntax alignment regularizes representation learning, leading to faster convergence and stronger performance on both syntactic and semantic evaluations — all within the strict low-resource boundaries of the BabyLM Strict-Small track (10M words).

Subword Pooling

The differentiable pooling matrix bridges the subword–word granularity gap, enabling word-level syntactic supervision to refine BERT's internal subword representations through gradient backpropagation via PT.

Layered Vocab Filtering

Overlaying POS-guided vocabulary masks onto MLM logits reduces prediction entropy by ~6×, enabling robust vocabulary convergence even under strict small-data budgets where the model has limited exposure to rare words.

Multi-Task Co-tuning

In soft masking mode, the semantic MLM loss co-tunes the POS classifier through a differentiable gradient pathway, creating a virtuous cycle where better syntax predictions improve word predictions and vice versa.

Low-Resource Effectiveness

With only 10M words of training data, our best model (Layered Soft) achieves 72.4% BLiMP and 75.1% GLUE — a +3.3pp and +1.6pp improvement over the BASEBERT baseline, validating the syntax-as-prior hypothesis.
